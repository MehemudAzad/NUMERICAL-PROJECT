\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Diffusion image sampling is an initial-value problem: one integrates the
probability-flow ODE from noise to data, paying one neural-network evaluation
(\NFE) per stage. Lu et al.\ (\emph{DPM-Solver}, NeurIPS 2022) accelerate this by
solving the linear half of the ODE exactly, and validate the result with FID --- an
image-quality score. We grade the same solvers by the standards of numerical
analysis --- measured convergence order, stability at the stiff boundary
$t\!\to\!0$, and error per \NFE\ --- on three testbeds of decreasing reference
exactness (an anisotropic Gaussian with a closed-form trajectory, a point mixture
referenced against \textsc{dop853}, and the real CIFAR-10 DDPM network referenced
against a fine grid of itself), with three solver arms that separate DPM-Solver's
two ideas.

The order theorem holds where its hypotheses do: on the exact-score testbeds, in
the asymptotic regime, every solver reaches its textbook order to within $0.094$.
But at the budgets the method is actually used at (10--120 \NFE, down to
$t_{\text{end}}=10^{-3}$) no solver is in that regime --- \emph{even with an exact
score}, measured order misses theory by up to $1.9$, most of the $2.8$ shortfall
seen on the real network. Explicit steppers in $t$ do destabilise near the data,
but because of \emph{curvature}, not stiffness: never on the linear testbed at any
condition number, while on the curved mixture RK4 in $t$ has a real stability
limit that the $\lam$-reparameterisation removes. The paper's unexplained Table~6
anomaly --- order~3 worse than order~1 at ${\sim}10$ \NFE\ --- reproduces on the
paper's own checkpoint: at matched \NFE, DPM-Solver-3 overtakes DPM-Solver-1 at
$14$ \NFE. Finally, trajectory accuracy and image quality disagree: at 10 \NFE\
DPM-Solver-2 lands $2.3\times$ further from the converged image than
DPM-Solver-1 yet scores $1.8\times$ better FID, because it produces a sharp but
\emph{different} picture. We also identify when DPM-Solver's exact linear
treatment helps (concentrated data) and when it hurts (spread-out data), and we
report the predictions we got wrong.
\end{abstract}

\section{The problem, in numerical terms}
% =====================================================================

A diffusion model generates an image by starting from Gaussian noise and
following a deterministic flow to the data manifold. That flow is the
\emph{probability-flow ODE}, and evaluating its right-hand side requires one
forward pass of a large neural network. The network call is essentially the
entire cost of sampling, so the governing question is how few evaluations one
can afford before the trajectory leaves the manifold. The unit of cost is the
\NFE, the number of function evaluations.

The base paper's contribution is a change of variables. The right-hand side
splits into a linear part, which is exactly integrable in closed form, and a
neural part, which is not. Classical explicit schemes approximate both at once
and waste error budget on the half that was free. DPM-Solver treats the linear
half exactly and spends its entire approximation on the hard half, reaching good
samples in 10--20 \NFE\ where prior samplers needed 100--250.

The paper proves an order theorem for this scheme and validates it with FID
scores on generated images. FID is a distributional measure of image quality; it
is not a measure of how accurately an ODE was integrated. Three quantities a
numerical analyst would ask for are therefore measured only indirectly, if at all:

\begin{description}
\item[Order.] Halving the step size $h$ should reduce the error by $2^{p}$ for a
$p$th-order method. The paper proves $p$ but does not measure it against a
known-correct trajectory.
\item[Stability.] The ODE stiffens as $t\!\to\!0$ because $\sigma(t)\!\to\!0$.
\S4.2 of the paper suggests, citing the exponential-integrator literature, that
classical explicit methods ``may suffer from unstable numerical issues for large
step size''; it does not measure a stability limit.
\item[Cost.] A higher-order method is more accurate per step but calls the
network more times per step. Whether it wins after dividing out is an empirical
question.
\end{description}

This report supplies all three, and then puts the numerical verdict next to the
image-quality one. An earlier draft compared measurements taken under different
settings; a review caught it, and the controls in \S\S4--7 replace those
comparisons. Where a conclusion changed as a result, the ledger in \S8 says so.

\section{Mathematical setting}
% =====================================================================

We use the variance-preserving linear schedule of the base paper, with
$\beta_0 = 0.1$, $\beta_1 = 20$, and $t$ running from $T=1$ down to a small
$t_{\text{end}}$:
\begin{align}
\log \alpha(t) &= -\tfrac{\beta_1-\beta_0}{4}t^2 - \tfrac{\beta_0}{2}t,
&\sigma(t) &= \sqrt{1-\alpha(t)^2}, \\
\beta(t) &= \beta_0 + t(\beta_1-\beta_0),
&f(t) &= \tfrac{\mathrm{d}\log\alpha}{\mathrm{d}t} = -\tfrac{1}{2}\beta(t),
\qquad g^2(t) = \beta(t).
\end{align}

The half log-SNR $\lam(t) = \log\alpha(t) - \log\sigma(t)$ is the natural time
variable for this problem. It is \emph{strictly decreasing} in $t$: $t=1$ gives
the most negative $\lam$, and $t\!\to\!0$ sends $\lam\!\to\!+\infty$. Its inverse
has a cancellation-free closed form,
\begin{equation}
t(\lam) = \frac{2u}{\sqrt{\beta_0^2 + 2(\beta_1-\beta_0)u} + \beta_0},
\qquad u = \log\!\left(e^{-2\lam}+1\right).
\end{equation}

Writing $\eps(x,t)$ for the noise-prediction model --- a network in general, an
exact formula on our analytic tiers --- the same trajectory admits three
discretisable forms, and these define our three solver arms:
\begin{align}
\text{(A)}\quad &\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t} = f(t)\,x + \frac{g^2(t)}{2\sigma(t)}\,\eps(x,t),
  &&\text{uniform grid in } t, \label{eq:armA}\\[2pt]
\text{(B)}\quad &\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}\lam} = \hat\sigma(\lam)^2 x - \hat\sigma(\lam)\,\eps(x,t(\lam)),
  &&\text{uniform grid in } \lam, \label{eq:armB}\\[2pt]
\text{(C)}\quad &x_i = \frac{\alpha_i}{\alpha_{i-1}}x_{i-1} - \sigma_i\left(e^{h}-1\right)\eps(x_{i-1},t_{i-1}),
  &&h = \lam_i - \lam_{i-1}, \label{eq:armC}
\end{align}
with $\hat\alpha(\lam) = (1+e^{-2\lam})^{-1/2}$ and
$\hat\sigma(\lam) = (1+e^{2\lam})^{-1/2}$. Equations~\eqref{eq:armA}
and~\eqref{eq:armB} are the same ODE in different coordinates;
\eqref{eq:armC} is DPM-Solver-1, exact on the linear part. Comparing A with B
isolates the reparameterisation alone; comparing B with C isolates the exact
linear treatment alone. Figure~\ref{fig:arms} makes the distinction concrete.

We implement $e^h-1$ as \texttt{expm1(h)} throughout. The naive form loses
relative precision catastrophically for small $h$, which is exactly the regime a
convergence study measures.

\section{Experimental design}
% =====================================================================

\subsection{Three tiers}

To measure error one needs the right answer, which for a real generated image
does not exist. We therefore build deliberately simplified problems whose exact
solution is available, and step up in realism (Figure~\ref{fig:tiers}).

\paragraph{Tier 1 --- anisotropic Gaussian.}
For data $\sim\mathcal N(0,\operatorname{diag}(s))$ the ODE decouples into $d$
independent scalar linear ODEs. Both the score and the entire trajectory are
closed form:
\begin{equation}
\eps^{*}(x,t)_j = \frac{\sigma(t)\,x_j}{v_j(t)},\qquad
x_j(t) = x_j(T)\sqrt{\frac{v_j(t)}{v_j(T)}},\qquad
v_j(t) = \alpha(t)^2 s_j + \sigma(t)^2 .
\end{equation}
The stiffness knob is the condition number $\kappa = s_{\max}/s_{\min}$, which
we sweep over five decades. Because the linear case decouples, dimension changes
nothing --- we sweep $\kappa$, not $d$.

\paragraph{Tier 2 --- Gaussian/point mixture.}
Replacing the single Gaussian by a weighted point cloud $\mu_1,\dots,\mu_K$
keeps the score closed form but makes it genuinely nonlinear in $x$: the
posterior mean becomes a softmax over modes,
\begin{equation}
r_k \propto w_k \exp\!\left(-\frac{\lVert x-\alpha(t)\mu_k\rVert^2}{2\sigma(t)^2}\right),
\quad \hat x_0 = \sum_k r_k \mu_k,
\quad \eps^{*}(x,t) = \frac{x-\alpha(t)\hat x_0}{\sigma(t)} .
\end{equation}
We place $K=8$ modes on a Swiss-roll curve. There is no algebraic trajectory, so
the reference is SciPy's \textsc{dop853} at \texttt{rtol}~$=10^{-13}$,
\texttt{atol}~$=10^{-14}$, verified converged by recomputation at $10^{-11}$
(agreement $6.9\times10^{-13}$, far below every measured error). The
responsibilities are computed with \texttt{logsumexp}; a naive
$\exp$-then-normalise overflows long before the ratios cease to be well defined.

\paragraph{Tier 3 --- the real network.}
\texttt{google/ddpm-cifar10-32}, $d = 3072$, on a Kaggle T4 --- the same DDPM
checkpoint as the paper's discrete-time CIFAR-10 experiments. The reference is a
201-\NFE\ DPM-Solver-3 run of the \emph{same} checkpoint from the \emph{same}
$x_T$, so what is measured remains discretisation error rather than model error.
\S7 adds what this metric cannot see: the samples themselves, and their FID.

A convergence study depends on \emph{where} and \emph{at what budget} it is run,
and this report uses two settings deliberately (Table~\ref{tab:protocols}).

The asymptotic protocol avoids the region near the data, where the problem is
hardest, and starts each sweep past the solver's pre-asymptotic regime --- the
right conditions for checking that an implementation has the order the theorem
promises. The practitioner protocol is Tier~3's: a network is expensive, and
nobody samples it at 500 \NFE. An earlier draft of this report compared Tier-1/2
slopes from the first protocol with Tier-3 slopes from the second, and attributed
the difference to the network; \S5.2 corrects that.

\subsection{Conventions}

Three decisions were fixed before any measurement and never revisited.
\textbf{Float64 everywhere} on Tiers 1--2: a float32 error floor near $10^{-7}$
would destroy the order fits. \textbf{Every run seeded.} \textbf{One results
schema} --- every experiment appends a row
\texttt{(tier, testbed, arm, solver, order, kappa, h, nfe, err\_l2, diverged,
seed)} to a CSV, so all tables concatenate without adapters.

Two accounting rules matter throughout. \NFE\ is \emph{not} step count: RK4
spends four network calls per step, DPM-Solver-$k$ spends $k$, and AB2 spends one
after startup. Every cross-arm comparison uses \emph{measured} \NFE, obtained
from a call counter wrapped around the right-hand side rather than assumed from a
formula. And order is defined in $h$, not \NFE; since arms measure $h$ in
different units, $h$ is used only \emph{within} an arm.

\subsection{Fitting an order}

A real convergence curve has three regions: pre-asymptotic at large $h$ where
theory does not yet apply, a straight middle, and a round-off floor at small $h$.
Fitting one line through all points averages across regimes and reports the wrong
number. We instead search every contiguous log--log subwindow and keep the one
that is both straight and wide, and we report the fit window and $R^2$ beside
every slope. This procedure is not infallible --- \S\ref{sec:tier3} documents a
case where it must be told about the floor explicitly.

\subsection{Validation: Gate G1}

Section 4.1 of the paper proves DDIM and DPM-Solver-1 are algebraically the same
update. Two independent implementations must therefore agree to machine
precision. Ours agree to $5.5\times10^{-16}$ against a $10^{-14}$ bar, and
DPM-Solver-1 measures at order $0.99$. Everything downstream was gated on this.
Separately, the Tier-1 closed-form trajectory satisfies the ODE to $\sim10^{-11}$
by finite difference and matches an independent \textsc{dop853} integration to
$10^{-12}$; our schedule agrees with the vendored
\texttt{NoiseScheduleVP('linear')} to $10^{-11}$, so all three arms provably
share one schedule.

We use the authors' own solver file, unmodified and pinned to a commit, driven by
an analytic oracle. This tests the \emph{published implementation} with zero
model error rather than a reimplementation of it.

\subsection{Stability: absent on the linear problem, real on the curved one}

Section 4.2 of the paper suggests that explicit methods on this semi-linear ODE
destabilise at large steps near $t\!\to\!0$. We measured it directly, bisecting on
step count for the coarsest grid that stays bounded, for all five steppers in
arms A and B, on both analytic tiers down to $t_{\text{end}} = 10^{-3}$.

\paragraph{Tier 1: no instability at any $\kappa$.} Across
$\kappa\in\{1,10,10^2,10^3,10^4\}$, every stepper in both arms is already stable on
the coarsest grid tried (2 steps: $h \approx 0.4995$ in $t$, $4.79$ in $\lam$), so
the bisection never engages; stress-testing to $\kappa = 10^{8}$ and
$t_{\text{end}} = 10^{-6}$ changes nothing. (The bisection itself is verified
against a synthetic linear ODE with a closed-form instability threshold.) The
reason is structural. On a uniform-$t$ grid the node nearest the boundary sits at
$t_{\text{end}}+h$, and the stability product $h\cdot J(t_{\text{end}}+h)$, with $J$
the local Jacobian, never exceeds ${\approx}\,0.9$ for any $h$ or $\kappa$ ---
under explicit Euler's threshold of 2 --- because $\sigma(t)^2 \sim \beta_0 t$ near
the boundary makes the growing stiffness and the grid's shrinking distance to
$t_{\text{end}}$ very nearly cancel. The stiffness knob $\kappa$ cannot defeat this:
for $s_{\min}\ll\sigma^2$ the local rate is capped by $\sigma(t)^2$, not by
$s_{\min}$.

\paragraph{Tier 2: a real limit, and only in $t$.} The curved mixture behaves
differently (Figure~\ref{fig:stab2}). Its Jacobian grows without that cancellation:
between modes the posterior weights switch ever more sharply as $\sigma\to0$, and
at $t = 10^{-3}$ the spectral norm of $\partial(\text{rhs}_t)/\partial x$ is 545,
against 0.54 on Tier 1 --- three orders of magnitude. Arm-A RK4 now has a genuine
stability limit, $h_{\max} = 0.1998$: stable from 5 steps, divergent at 2--4. A
stricter divergence rule (twice the data scale instead of ten times
$\lVert x_T\rVert$) gives the same limit. The other arm-A steppers stay stable at the
coarsest grid, and the reason is where they sample: RK4 is the only one whose
final stage evaluates the right-hand side at the \emph{end} of the step --- on the
last step, at $t_{\text{end}}$ itself, where the Jacobian peaks. In arm B every
stepper is stable at every grid tried.

So the paper's \S4.2 claim is \emph{narrowed}, not refuted: explicit steppers in
$t$ do destabilise near the data, but the cause is curvature of the data
distribution, not the stiffness parameter the claim --- and our original
prediction --- points to; and the $\lam$-reparameterisation removes it.

\subsection{When does the exact linear treatment help?}

DPM-Solver's premise is that integrating the linear term exactly and
approximating only $\eps$ is always the better split. Under the practitioner
protocol the two analytic tiers disagree about that:

\begin{itemize}
\item On Tier~1 ($\kappa = 10$), DPM-Solver-1 and -2 lose to the classical stepper
of the same order, in either coordinate, at every budget from 10 to 120 \NFE: at
20 \NFE, DPM-Solver-2 errs by $0.133$, RK2 in $t$ by $0.034$, RK2 in $\lam$ by
$0.0089$; at 10 \NFE, DPM-Solver-1 errs by $0.425$ against Euler-in-$t$'s $0.092$.
DPM-Solver-3 loses to RK4 in $\lam$ throughout, and beats RK4 in $t$ only above
${\sim}23$ \NFE.
\item On Tier~2 it wins where the paper says it should: at 12 \NFE, DPM-Solver-2
errs by $0.084$ against $0.23$ (RK2 in $\lam$) and $0.34$ (RK2 in $t$), and
DPM-Solver-3 by $0.049$ against $0.30$ (RK4 in $\lam$) and $14.8$ (RK4 in $t$: 3 steps,
past its 5-step stability limit, \S4.2).
\end{itemize}

The difference is how \emph{concentrated} the data is, and it has a two-line
explanation. DPM-Solver's local error is driven by how much $\eps$ varies along
the trajectory in $\lam$; a classical stepper's is driven by the whole right-hand
side. For data concentrated at a point ($s\to0$), $\eps = x/\sigma$ and the exact
flow is $x\propto\sigma$, so $\eps$ is \emph{constant} along the path and
DPM-Solver-1 is exact; substituting $e^{h} = (\alpha_i\sigma_{i-1})/(\sigma_i\alpha_{i-1})$
into Eq.~\eqref{eq:armC} gives $x_i = (\sigma_i/\sigma_{i-1})\,x_{i-1}$ identically.
For data as spread out as the noise ($s = 1$), $v\equiv1$, the whole right-hand side
of Eq.~\eqref{eq:armA} vanishes and every classical stepper is exact, while
$\eps = \hat\sigma(\lam)\,x$ still varies from 1 to 0 across the trajectory and
DPM-Solver-1 is not. Figure~\ref{fig:split} sweeps $s$ between the two: at 20 \NFE,
for $s < 10^{-2}$ DPM-Solver-1 beats Euler-in-$t$ at 83\% of points and
DPM-Solver-2 beats RK2-in-$t$ at all of them; at $s = 1$ the classical steppers are
exact to $10^{-16}$ while DPM-Solver-1 errs by $5.9\times10^{-3}$. Tier~1's
eigenvalues run up to $s = 1$; Tier~2 is a set of point masses. Natural images, whose
pixel-space covariance spectrum decays rapidly, put most of their directions at the
concentrated end, which is consistent with DPM-Solver's success on them --- though
we have not measured that spectrum here.

\section{Tier 3: the order theorem meets a real network}
\label{sec:tier3}

\subsection{One schedule, read off the checkpoint}

The CIFAR-10 checkpoint is a \emph{discrete-time} DDPM: 1000 $\beta$ values, with
$\log\alpha$ linearly interpolated between tabulated times. That is the same
VP-linear schedule discretised, and it is \emph{not} interchangeable with the
continuous form --- we measure $\max|\Delta\lam| = 4.74\times10^{-2}$ and a
$5.0\%$ discrepancy in $f$. Using continuous coefficients for arms A and B would
place a systematic $5\%$ error beneath every Tier-3 curve.

We therefore derive all three arms from the checkpoint's own schedule. Arm B
needs only $\hat\sigma(\lam)$ --- which follows from $\lam = \log\alpha-\log\sigma$
and $\alpha^2+\sigma^2=1$ alone, hence is exact for any variance-preserving
schedule --- and $t(\lam)$. Arm A needs $f$ and $g^2$, and here one identity does
all the work. Differentiating $\sigma^2 = 1-\alpha^2$ gives
$\sigma' = -\alpha^2 f/\sigma$, so
\begin{equation}
\lam' = f - \frac{\sigma'}{\sigma} = f\left(1+\frac{\alpha^2}{\sigma^2}\right) = \frac{f}{\sigma^2},
\qquad\text{hence}\qquad
\boxed{\;f = \sigma^2\lam',\qquad g^2 = -2\sigma^2\lam' = -2f.\;}
\label{eq:vpid}
\end{equation}
Taking \emph{both} coefficients from a single central difference of $\lam$ makes
$f/\lam' \equiv \sigma^2$ hold to $1.1\times10^{-16}$ rather than approximately
--- and that identity is exactly what makes \eqref{eq:armA} and \eqref{eq:armB}
the same ODE. Arms A and B therefore agree by construction; we verify it end to
end, integrating both at four resolutions and watching the gap fall from
$2.5\times10^{-4}$ to $3.9\times10^{-7}$.

\paragraph{On Assumption B.1.} An earlier draft attributed the Tier-3 shortfall to
the network violating the order theorem's smoothness hypothesis. That is hard to
defend: B.1 asks only that the $\lam$-derivatives of $\eps$ \emph{exist and be
continuous}, and a UNet with SiLU activations, normalisation and sinusoidal time
embeddings is a smooth function of its inputs. What a network can do is make those
derivatives \emph{large}, which pushes the start of the asymptotic regime to
smaller step sizes than any practical budget reaches. The theorem is about
$h\to0$; at 10--120 \NFE\ it is simply not the operative description, on any tier.

\section{The crossover: when does order 3 beat order 1?}
\label{sec:crossover}
% =====================================================================

Table 6 of the base paper reports, without explanation, that on this very
checkpoint (CIFAR-10 DDPM, discrete-time, $\epsilon = 10^{-3}$) DPM-Solver-3 is
far \emph{worse} than DPM-Solver-1 at 10 \NFE\ --- FID 24.37 (at an actual 9
\NFE) against 16.69 --- and far better by 12 \NFE\ (8.20 against 13.63). This is
the textbook pre-asymptotic picture: at large $h$ the cubic Taylor correction is

\paragraph{Our FID ranking matches the paper's.} At $\sim$10 \NFE\ the paper's three
headline comparisons all hold on our run: DPM-Solver-2 beats DPM-Solver-1 ($24.9$ vs

and it passes.

\paragraph{Trajectory accuracy and image quality disagree.} Among the five
samplers that produce an image at all at 10 \NFE, ranking by FID is the
\emph{exact reverse} of ranking by distance to the converged image: L2 orders them
RK2-in-$t$, DPM-Solver-1, DDIM, DPM-Solver-fast, DPM-Solver-2; FID orders them
DPM-Solver-2, DPM-Solver-fast, DDIM, DPM-Solver-1, RK2-in-$t$. DPM-Solver-2 lands

yet scores $1.8\times$ better FID; M9's independent 64-image batch agrees ($166.4$ vs
$75.7$). Figure~\ref{fig:grid} shows what happens, and suggests why: a few large
steps at high noise decide the picture's layout early and commit to it. DPM-Solver-2 then denoises crisply --- a
good sample, but not \emph{this} trajectory's sample --- while RK2 in $t$ stays on
the trajectory and leaves it noisy. FID rewards the first, L2 the second. By
$\sim$20 \NFE\ the two metrics largely agree again (rank correlation $+0.60$,
against $-1.00$ at 10 \NFE), and the best samplers are within
FID-5k noise of the converged run (two score marginally \emph{below} it).

This is the project's thesis in one table. ``DPM-Solver produces good images in 10
\NFE'' and ``DPM-Solver integrates the ODE accurately in 10 \NFE'' are different
claims; the paper's evidence is for the first. For the second, no sampler we ran
comes within a few gray levels of the converged image at 10 \NFE: the closest,
RK2 in $t$, is still 11 gray levels RMS away. Neither metric alone answers which sampler is best: one
asks for a plausible image, the other for the image this noise was meant to
become.

\paragraph{Caveats: absolute FID, and the sample-size floor.} Our absolute FIDs are
about three times the paper's --- even the converged run scores $16.5$ against the
paper's ${\sim}5.25$ --- so only rankings are compared. How much of that is the
sample size alone is measurable (notebook 11b, Figure~\ref{fig:floor}): real,
held-out CIFAR-10 test images, scored with the identical FID call, follow
$\mathrm{FID} \approx 3.0\times10^{4}/N$ almost exactly from 1,000 to 10,000
images. At 10,000 they give $3.15$, clean-fid's documented test-versus-train value,
which also validates the FID pipeline itself. At our 5,000 images the floor --- the
best FID any sampler could score in this setup --- is $5.9$; at the paper's 50,000
the same term is ${\approx}\,0.6$. Sample size therefore explains $5.2$ of the
$11.2$-point gap to the paper, a little under half. The other half is a real
difference: with the sample-size term removed, our converged samples score
${\approx}\,10.6$ against the paper's ${\approx}\,4.6$. Plausible causes are
clean-fid's port of the TensorFlow Inception network and the discrete-to-continuous
time conversion (the paper's ``Type-2 discrete'' against the vendored code's
interpolated schedule); we did not isolate them. Because the floor shifts every
sampler by the same amount, it changes no ranking in Table~\ref{tab:fid}.

Two smaller points. The FID samples were seeded from Python's per-process string
hash, so they are not bit-reproducible and differ between samplers; differences of a
few tenths are noise. The L2 columns use M11's own 64-image batch (seed 1); M9's
seed-0 batch reproduces them to within 3--9\%, with the same rankings.

\section{The ledger}
% =====================================================================

Predictions were written down before the measurements that test them: six in the
project guide, before any sweep ran, and eight in the review that led to M11 and
M12. Every verdict is computed from the results CSVs by
\texttt{notebooks/10\_report.ipynb} (\texttt{results/predictions\_ledger.csv}),
not asserted by hand. Reporting the misses is more useful than reporting only the
hits.

\paragraph{Departures from the proposal.} The efficiency frontier is reported in
\NFE\ rather than wall-clock (the paper's Table 7 shows runtime is linear in \NFE\
and near-identical across solvers, so wall-clock adds nothing). Tier 1 sweeps
$\kappa$ at fixed $d$ rather than $d = 2\dots100$, because the linear case
decouples and dimension changes nothing. Tier 2 uses a point mixture rather than a
continuous Swiss roll, which has no closed-form score. The reference tolerance is
$10^{-13}$ rather than $10^{-14}$, which SciPy refuses. Adaptive RK45 was
\textbf{dropped}: step size is not a free variable for an adaptive method, so
neither ``order versus $h$'' nor ``maximum stable $h$'' is defined on it; AB2
restores the multistep row instead. FID was cut at the week-2 gate and
\textbf{restored in a limited role}: FID-5k on six samplers, as an anchor to the
paper's Table 6 and a counterpoint to L2 --- never as the grading metric. Added
beyond the proposal: arm B, Gate G1, the crossover study at both matched step and
matched \NFE, the three-way Tier-3 error decomposition, the matched-protocol
controls, the stability study on a curved problem, the data-concentration sweep,
and the first sample images.

% =====================================================================
\section{Reproducibility}
% =====================================================================

The engine is plain Python under \texttt{src/}, unit-tested (129 tests), with no
GPU or plotting code. Notebooks import it, run the sweeps and write
\texttt{results/} and \texttt{figures/}. \texttt{run\_all.sh} reproduces every
laptop-side figure and CSV from a clean clone (notebooks 01--08, 10 and 12); it
skips notebooks 09, 11 and 11b, which need a CUDA GPU and run on a Kaggle T4, and
whose outputs are committed so that the report notebook reads them without a GPU.

One implementation note worth recording: the same integrator and the same
right-hand side definition march all three tiers. \texttt{integrate} is
backend-agnostic (float64 NumPy on Tiers 1--2, float32 CUDA tensors on Tier 3)
and the probability-flow ODE is written once, parameterised by a schedule object.
The claim that arms A and B mean the same thing across tiers is therefore
structural rather than a matter of careful duplication.

\section{Conclusion}
% =====================================================================

Judged as a numerical method, DPM-Solver holds up where its theory applies, and
the interesting findings are about where it does not.

On testbeds with analytic scores, in the asymptotic regime, every solver we tested
reaches its textbook order to within $0.1$, and the published code implements the
method correctly. But the order theorem is a statement about $h\to0$, and at the
budgets diffusion models are sampled at --- 10--120 \NFE, integrated down to
$t_{\text{end}}=10^{-3}$ --- no solver is in that regime, with or without a
network: exact scores already miss theory by up to $1.9$, most of the $2.8$ seen on
the network. At those budgets three other things decide which sampler wins.

\emph{Curvature.} It is what destabilises explicit steppers near the data --- not
the stiffness knob the paper's \S4.2 and our own prediction pointed to --- and it
moves the crossover at which order 3 starts to beat order 1 later, from $5$ \NFE\
on a linear problem to $14$ on the real network, where it reproduces the paper's
unexplained Table-6 anomaly. The $\lam$-reparameterisation removes the instability
and, for RK4, cuts error by one to four orders of magnitude on every tier.

\emph{How concentrated the data is.} DPM-Solver's exact linear treatment is exact
for a point mass and a handicap for data as spread out as the noise, where the
classical right-hand side cancels identically. Its success on images is
consistent with image data being concentrated; it is not unconditional.

\emph{What ``good'' means.} At 10 \NFE\ ranking samplers by closeness to the
converged image and ranking them by FID give exactly opposite orders among the ones
that produce an image at all. DPM-Solver's advantage at low \NFE\ is real, and our
FIDs rank it as the paper does; but it is an advantage in producing a
\emph{plausible} image, not in integrating the ODE. A practitioner who needs the
latter --- reproducible trajectories, interpolation in latent space, inversion ---
should budget on the order of 30--50 \NFE: by 20 \NFE\ the two rankings already
largely agree (rank correlation $+0.60$, against $-1.00$ at 10), and DPM-Solver-3
comes within one gray level of the converged image at about 36.
